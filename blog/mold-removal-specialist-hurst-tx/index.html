Choosing a Mold Removal Specialist in Hurst, TX

A good vetting takes about ten minutes and a few questions. Here is what to ask, what to verify and what should make you walk away.

By the Hurst Water Damage Pros team · 2026

☎ Call (817) 623-9267

Choose a mold removal specialist by verifying licenses, getting a written scope and keeping testing and cleanup in separate hands. Texas licenses mold assessors and mold remediators as two different roles, and TDLR publishes a license search anyone can use. Ask every company for its license numbers and check them yourself before signing anything.

Two roles, two licenses

RoleWhat they doLicense type
Mold assessorInspects, finds moisture, may sample, writes the protocol with cleanup criteria, checks results after cleanupMold assessment, licensed by TDLR
Mold remediatorFollows the protocol, builds containment, removes and cleans material, dries the spaceMold remediation, licensed by TDLR
General contractor or handymanRebuilds drywall, flooring and paint after clearanceNot a mold license

The split exists for a reason. A company that profits from cleanup has a reason to find a lot of mold, and one that tests its own work has a reason to pass it. When the assessor and the remediator are different companies, each checks the other.

Smaller jobs differ. Visible mold under 25 contiguous square feet may generally be cleaned by unlicensed people, and owners or managers of buildings with fewer than 10 dwelling units do not have to be licensed for mold work on that property. Beyond those lines, a protocol and a licensed remediator are the usual path.

A ten-minute vetting routine

  1. Ask for license numbersRequest the assessor or remediator number and the company name as filed. Look it up on the TDLR public license search and match names.
  2. Ask about the standardTesting and cleanup follow ANSI/IICRC S520. Ask the company whether its work follows it and how.
  3. Request a written scopeIt should list rooms, areas, methods, containment, materials to remove and what will be rebuilt by someone else.
  4. Ask about moistureThe company should explain how it found the source and what must be fixed before cleanup counts as done.
  5. Ask who clears the jobFind out who verifies that the work met the protocol's criteria, and whether you get documentation.
  6. Confirm insuranceAsk the company to show proof of liability coverage, and check it with the carrier named.

What a good written plan contains

Protocol or scope

A document that names the affected areas, the cleanup goals and the methods. For larger jobs it comes from a licensed assessor.

Containment plan

Sealed barriers, negative air or filtered air machines, controlled entry and how debris leaves the building.

Drying plan

How moisture will be measured and what readings count as dry.

Clearance step

Who inspects at the end, what criteria apply and what paperwork you receive.

TDI guidance for a mold claim says to use a licensed mold professional, expect an inspection confirming removal and that the cause was fixed, and get a certificate of removal. Even if you are not filing a claim, those are good expectations for any job.

Finally, trust how a company answers. Clear, patient answers about license numbers, standards and limits are a good sign. Vague replies, a rush to start or irritation at being asked to put things in writing are reasons to keep looking.

Ask also about the order of work. Cleanup that starts before the moisture source is fixed, or before drying is under way, tends to be redone. A sensible plan puts the water fix first, the removal second, the drying third and the clearance last.

Cheap is not the same as thin. A fair bid shows its math in plain terms: how much area, which materials come out, how long the containment stays up and how many days of drying follow. When two bids differ widely, compare the scopes line by line before comparing totals, because the lower one often leaves out containment or drying.

Red flags

  • No license number, or a number that does not match the company name on TDLR's search.
  • A verbal quote with no written scope.
  • Pressure to sign the same day or a story that you must act within hours.
  • A test offered as a giveaway to lead into a cleanup sale.
  • The same company inspecting, selling the cleanup and declaring the job clean.
  • No mention of finding or fixing the moisture source.
  • A promise that mold will never come back. Nobody can honestly say that.

Mold clearance for a home purchase

Some buyers learn that a lender will not fund a purchase until mold is removed. Lender rules differ and no company can promise a lender's decision, so start by asking the lender or title company what documentation it requires.

Commonly, parties look for an assessor's findings, a written remediation scope, evidence the moisture source was addressed and clearance paperwork once the work is done. Timelines depend on the size of the job, drying and any rebuild, so share the closing date with the company early.

Water first, mold second

A mold specialist can only succeed in a dry building. If the water is still present, a leak is unrepaired or materials remain wet, drying comes first through water mitigation by qualified crews. Ask whether the company can handle that step or coordinates with someone who can.

Ask, too, who does the rebuild. Mold crews remove and clean. Replacement drywall, flooring and paint typically follow after clearance, and the scope should say who does what.

What restoration can and cannot do

A qualified company can remove affected material, clean what can be cleaned, dry the structure and document the result. It cannot promise mold will not return, diagnose illness or tell you what exposure might mean for a person. Those questions belong to a physician.

Costs depend on the area affected, the materials, containment needs, drying days and equipment, and the rebuild scope. Scope and price should be explained before work starts. For questions on how a job could run, call the mold remediation team and bring your list of questions.
