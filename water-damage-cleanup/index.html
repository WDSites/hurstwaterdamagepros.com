What is in the water decides what happens to your things

Cleanup starts with one question: where did the water come from? The answer sets the water category, and the category sets how much has to be thrown out. A clean supply line and a sewer backup might leave the same puddle, but they are very different jobs.

When standing water and soaked rooms cannot wait

Call right away if sewage or dirty water is involved, if wet carpet is already smelling, or if the wet area is larger than a single room. Same-day dispatch is available when crews are free, and after-hours calls are answered the same as daytime calls.

Mold needs moisture, and TDI notes it can start within about a day. A quick call keeps a cleanup job from turning into a mold remediation job later.

Electrical and sewage safety

If water has reached an electrical panel, outlets or sewage is coming up from a drain, stay out of that area. Cut power only if you can do it without standing in water.

Call 911 or the utility for electrical or gas danger before calling the restoration line.

How a cleanup job moves

  1. Walk the home and testCrews check the water category, what the water touched and how far it spread, using moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras.
  2. Remove water and debrisExtraction units pull remaining water, and ruined carpet pad, insulation and drywall are bagged and carried out.
  3. Sort belongingsItems are separated into keep, clean and discard, with photos taken for your records.
  4. Sanitize and cleanHard surfaces and framing that stay are cleaned with products matched to the water category.
  5. Deodorize and dryOdors are treated at the source, and air movers and dehumidifiers finish pulling moisture out.

What gets thrown out and what can be saved

Porous materials soak up water and whatever was in it. Hard, sealed surfaces can usually be cleaned and kept. The category of water and how long the material stayed wet decide the call.

Usually saved

Solid wood furniture, glass, metal, plastic, sealed tile and concrete, if the water was Category 1 or 2 and they are cleaned promptly.

Often discarded

Carpet pad, insulation, particleboard and drywall after contaminated water, plus mattresses and upholstery from Category 3.

Case by case

Hardwood, rugs, books and photos. Quick drying can save some, while sewage ends the discussion.

Keep for your adjuster

Photograph damaged items first, and keep them until the adjuster has seen them if you plan to file a claim.

Sanitizing and deodorizing

A musty or sour smell means moisture or bacteria is still in the building. Perfume sprays only hide it. Cleanup finds the source of the odor, removes the material that holds it and treats the surface that remains.

For contaminated water, hard surfaces get cleaned and then sanitized in that order. Crews wear protective gear and keep the work area separate from the rest of the house. Work follows ANSI/IICRC S500, the published standard for water damage restoration, and homeowners can ask any company whether its crews follow it.

How cleanup fits with the rest of the job

Cleanup and drying overlap. The same visit that clears debris usually sets the water mitigation equipment that dries framing and subfloor. When pooled water is still deep at the start, emergency water removal comes first. After the structure is dry, water damage repair handles new drywall, flooring and paint.

Flooding from storms or creeks is a different case with its own rules, covered under flood damage restoration.

What drives the price of a cleanup

No two cleanups cost the same, and these five things explain why:

  • Water category, because Category 3 needs more removal and protective steps.
  • Affected square footage, which sets labor and the amount of debris.
  • Materials involved, since carpet, laminate, hardwood and drywall are handled differently.
  • Drying days and equipment needed before the home is clean and dry.
  • Rebuild scope, meaning how much of the house has to be replaced afterward.

The scope and price are explained before work starts, and you can ask for a real number by calling.

Insurance questions after cleanup

Texas Department of Insurance guidance says most homeowners and renters policies cover sudden, accidental water damage but usually not gradual leaks or seepage. Standard home policies exclude flood damage. Check your policy and ask your insurer before you decide what to discard.

Tell your company as soon as you can, take photos or video before cleanup, and make temporary repairs only. Hold off on permanent repairs until the insurer has inspected.
